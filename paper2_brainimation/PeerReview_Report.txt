REVIEWER 1 (Simulated Perspective: Kara D. Federmeier, Ph.D.)
Recommendation: Accept with Minor Revisions
================================================================================

Overview and General Appreciation

This manuscript describes BrainImation, a zero-install, browser-based live-coding environment that links four generations of consumer Muse headbands (Muse 2016, Muse 2, Classic Muse S, and the newer Muse S Athena) to the p5.js graphics and p5.sound audio synthesis libraries. I really enjoyed reading this paper and exploring the architecture of the tool. Anyone who has taught an undergraduate cognitive neuroscience or psychophysiology laboratory course knows how quickly a two-hour lab session can be derailed by driver installations, Bluetooth pairing quirks, LabStreamingLayer socket drops, and Python package conflicts. By moving the entire acquisition, simulation, signal conditioning, and visual/auditory rendering loop into a single browser tab—and pairing it with a 226-sketch visual catalogue and a hardware-free simulator that students can use at home on their own laptops—the author has built a genuinely useful pedagogical resource.

What I appreciate most about this revision is its grounded, educational focus. Rather than overselling consumer dry-electrode headbands as a replacement for shielded research laboratories, the manuscript honestly frames BrainImation as a constructionist learning studio where students learn by building, breaking, and inspecting real-time physiological code. Moreover, bringing electrical brain activity (EEG), optical hemodynamics (PPG and dual-distance fNIRS), cardiac pulsatility, and 3D head kinematics under a single shared JavaScript object (window.eegData) is a wonderful way to help students think about the brain as part of an integrated body and vascular system rather than an isolated electrical box.

My comments below are entirely constructive and require no new data collection. They focus on (1) helping instructors and students understand how reference electrode placement and sensor geometry on a forehead headband shape the ERP and optical signals they observe, (2) clarifying how the multimodal brain-heart-vascular modules can be used to teach physiological time scales and artifacts, and (3) sharpening a few presentation details in the student evaluation and figures.


Section 1. Pedagogical Framing of Sensor Geometry, Reference Placement, and Physiological Time Scales

1.1. Explaining the Forehead Reference (Fpz) and Electrode Geometry for Classroom ERPs (Section 2.2 and Section 4.2):
When students transition from textbook ERP chapters (where visual N170, oddball P300, or semantic N400 components are typically recorded relative to an average mastoid or earlobe reference) to a Muse headband, the single biggest conceptual surprise is the reference geometry. On Muse headbands, the active reference (REF) and Driven Right Leg (DRL) electrodes sit on the forehead (near Fpz), while the posterior electrodes (TP9 and TP10) sit behind the ears near the mastoids, and AF7 and AF8 sit on the lateral forehead. Because an EEG channel always measures the voltage difference between the active site and the reference site, recording TP9/TP10 relative to a forehead reference effectively inverts the classic mastoid-referenced vertex/parietal dipole! This is actually a fantastic teaching moment in a cognitive neuroscience lab. Please add a short paragraph in Section 4.2 (or Section 6) explaining how the forehead reference (Fpz) and the four recording sites (TP9, AF7, AF8, TP10) relate to standard 10-20 laboratory montages, and note which channels students typically inspect for visual N170 vs. oddball P300 effects on the headband.

1.2. Teaching Physiological Time Scales Across Electrical, Cardiac, Optical, and Kinematic Streams (Section 3.2 and Section 4.3):
One of the most exciting additions in this version of BrainImation is the side-by-side availability of 256 Hz EEG, 64 Hz dual-distance optical PPG/fNIRS, and 52 Hz 6-DOF head motion. For students, a major conceptual hurdle in cognitive neuroscience is understanding why different physiological measures unfold on vastly different time scales—millisecond phase-locked voltage deflections in ERPs (50 to 800 ms), sub-second to second envelope changes in oscillatory band power and cardiac R-R intervals, and slow 4 to 6 second hemodynamic lags in prefrontal oxyhemoglobin (Delta[HbO2]) and deoxyhemoglobin (Delta[HHb]). You already have a sketch in the Multimodal Studio (neurovascular coupling and heartbeat-evoked potentials) that touches on this. Please add two or three sentences in Section 3.2 or Section 4.3 highlighting how instructors can use these multimodal sketches to help students compare fast electrophysiological dynamics (milliseconds) against slower neurovascular coupling (seconds) within the same coding environment.

1.3. Clarifying What Students Learn from the Heartbeat-Evoked Potential (HEP) and Multimodal Sketches (Section 3.2):
In Section 3.2, you briefly mention the online Heartbeat-Evoked Potential (HEP) sketch that epochs 256 Hz EEG traces time-locked to PPG systolic peaks. From a psychophysiological teaching perspective, locking EEG epochs to an endogenous biological event (the optical pulse peak at the forehead) rather than an external screen flash is a great way to teach students about interoception, pulse-wave transit time (the delay between the cardiac R-wave in the chest and the optical pulse arrival at the forehead), and volume-conducted cardiac/pulsatile artifacts. Please add one or two sentences in Section 3.2 noting that locking EEG to the forehead PPG peak illustrates both endogenous event-locked averaging and the pulse-transit delay between the heart and the forehead vasculature.


Section 2. Student Evaluation and Classroom Implementation Details

2.1. Disaggregating Feature Usage Between Cohort 1 (Neurofeedback) and Cohort 2 (ERPs) in the Text (Section 5):
In Section 5 and Figure 4B, executable feature adoption is reported as a percentage of all N = 69 examination submissions combined (for example, 44.9% for eegData.alpha and 42.0% for online ERP averaging). Because the N = 69 archive is composed of two distinct assignments with different goals—Cohort 1 (N = 40 continuous neurofeedback games) and Cohort 2 (N = 29 online 4-channel ERP paradigms)—pooling the denominator across all 69 files makes the adoption rates look near 40-45% even when nearly every student in a given cohort used the relevant feature for their assignment! In fact, as you note in passing for alpha (31/40 = 77.5% of Cohort 1) and as Table 2 shows for ERPs (29/29 = 100% of Cohort 2), students matched their signal modality closely to the assignment prompt. Please revise the paragraph in Section 5 so that for the primary EEG features (alpha, beta, attention, meditation, and raw 256 Hz ERP buffers), you state the percentage within the relevant assignment cohort alongside the overall pooled percentage.

2.2. Describing How Instructors Use the Visual Catalogue and Simulator in a Typical Weekly Lab Sequence (Section 3.2 or Section 5):
Readers who teach laboratory courses will want to know how the 226-sketch Visual Catalogue and the built-in simulator fit into a semester schedule. Please add a few sentences in Section 5 (or at the end of Section 3.2) describing the practical classroom workflow: for example, how many lab sessions students spend modifying the 32-line scaffolds before starting their open-ended Cohort 1 and Cohort 2 projects, how students use the simulator outside of class hours to debug state machines and visual layouts before testing on live headbands in lab, and how URL sketch links (?sketch=) are used by teaching assistants to run and grade student submissions.

2.3. Clarifying the Role of the 2022 and 2025 Archival Galleries for Student Peer Learning (Section 3.2 and Table 2):
The inclusion of 121 past student projects (95 from the 2025 Neurotech class and 26 from the 2022 NeuroArt gallery) directly inside the live Visual Catalogue is a lovely pedagogical touch. Please briefly clarify in Section 5 whether the N = 69 graded examination submissions analyzed in Figure 4 are a subset of the 95 sketches in the 2025 Neurotech Class Catalogue or a separate examination cohort, so readers can easily connect the rows of Table 2.


Section 3. Figure and Table Clarity

3.1. Figure 2D Caption and Bar Labels (Page 6):
In Figure 2D (Multimodal API Coverage Across Catalogue, N = 226), the bars clearly show the percentage and count of all 226 catalogue sketches using each feature (for example, 53.5% [121/226] for eegData.alpha and 15.5% [35/226] for 52 Hz IMU). In an earlier draft of the caption, the text mentioned both the 36 core scaffolds and the 226 total sketches, whereas the updated plot cleanly shows the full 226-sketch catalogue. The current Figure 2D caption is accurate, but please add a brief note in Section 3.2 pointing out the top three or four modalities across the 226 sketches so the prose directly echoes Figure 2D.

3.2. Figure 4C and 4D Readability of Student Canvas Screenshots (Page 12):
In Figure 4C (Catch 20 Cones neurofeedback game) and Figure 4D (4-Channel Face vs. Object ERP averager), the dark-background canvas screenshots are authentic examples of student work, but the small status text inside the screenshots is naturally tiny when printed on a single page. Please add one sentence to the Figure 4 caption describing what the visual elements in Panels C and D show (for example, noting the four colored channel traces and condition legend in Panel D, and the falling cone, catcher paddle, and bottom alpha/beta status bar in Panel C) so readers do not need to squint at the in-canvas text.

Overview and General Assessment

In this paper, Mathewson presents BrainImation, a browser-based coding environment that allows students and researchers to record, simulate, process, and visualize EEG, optical PPG/fNIRS, and 6-axis IMU signals using JavaScript and p5.js. I am very enthusiastic about this work. Teaching ERP and EEG signal processing to undergraduate students is notoriously challenging because the mathematical operations—Fourier transforms, windowing, baseline correction, time-locked ensemble averaging, artifact rejection, and adaptive regression—often feel like abstract black boxes when students only click buttons in a GUI. Having students read and modify a 15-line loop that actually sums pre-stimulus baseline samples, subtracts the baseline mean, and updates a running ERP average across trials is one of the best ways I have seen to demystify how ERP averaging actually works.

I also want to commend the author for the candid, straightforward tone of the manuscript. The paper is careful not to claim that a four-channel dry headband running in a web browser at 60 FPS replaces a research-grade active-electrode ERP system with hardware trigger lines. Instead, it clearly explains the engineering and physiological constraints—including WebBluetooth packet burstiness, 60 Hz display frame jitter (15 to 45 ms), dry forehead artifact susceptibility, and the synthetic nature of the algorithm verification benchmarks.

I have a set of detailed, constructive suggestions below. None of them require collecting new data or rewriting the software; they are aimed at making the signal-processing explanations, ERP plotting conventions, and quantitative benchmark descriptions as rigorous and pedagogically clear as possible for instructors and students.


Section 1. ERP Averaging, Noise Scaling, and Trigger Jitter Mechanics

1.1. Reconciling Theoretical 1/sqrt(K) Noise Scaling with Single-Realization Baseline RMS in Section 4.2 (Page 9):
In Section 4.2, the text states that a single simulated trial (K = 1) has a background noise level of 17.4 microvolts (SNR = 0.55), averaging across K = 5 trials reduces the noise to 5.7 microvolts (SNR = 1.66), and averaging across K = 20 trials "reduces the noise by sqrt(20) to 2.7 microvolts (SNR = 3.48)." When I checked the arithmetic, 17.4 microvolts divided by sqrt(5) is 7.78 microvolts (compared to 5.7 microvolts), and 17.4 microvolts divided by sqrt(20) is 3.89 microvolts (compared to 2.7 microvolts). Why did the empirical baseline RMS in your simulation drop slightly faster than 17.4 / sqrt(K)? Because in a single simulation run, the RMS of a short 51-sample (-200 to 0 ms) pre-stimulus baseline window fluctuates from trial to trial due to random phase alignment of the simulated alpha/theta/delta waves; trial 1 happened to have a slightly higher baseline RMS (17.4 microvolts) than the average single-trial expectation (~12-13 microvolts). Please clarify this in Section 4.2 by stating both the theoretical 1/sqrt(K) expected reduction (a 4.47-fold reduction at K = 20) and the empirical baseline RMS observed in the plotted realization (17.4, 5.7, and 2.7 microvolts), so students and instructors who check the math with a calculator see why the single-run numbers differ slightly from 17.4 / sqrt(20).

1.2. Connecting Baseline RMS Noise to Standardized Measurement Error (SME) for Teaching Data Quality (Section 4.2 or Section 6):
In Section 4.2, data quality across K = 1, 5, and 20 trials is quantified using the RMS of the pre-stimulus baseline period (-200 to 0 ms) and the ratio of the peak P300 amplitude (9.5 microvolts) to that baseline RMS. Using pre-stimulus baseline RMS as a proxy for noise is a classic and intuitive way to introduce SNR to undergraduates. In modern ERP methodology, we also encourage students to think about the standard error of the actual amplitude score across trials—the Standardized Measurement Error (SME; Luck et al., 2021)—because low-frequency drift can be near zero in the pre-stimulus baseline (where it was just forced to zero by baseline subtraction!) and grow larger at +300 to +500 ms post-stimulus. Please add two or three sentences in Section 4.2 or Section 6 noting that while pre-stimulus baseline RMS provides a simple real-time classroom indicator of noise reduction, instructors can also have students compute trial-to-trial variability (standard error across trials at the P300 time window, or SME) to see how baseline subtraction affects noise differently during the baseline vs. post-stimulus window.

1.3. Quantifying the Low-Pass Filtering Effect of 15 to 45 ms Browser Trigger Jitter (Section 6, Page 13):
In Section 6, you rightly point out that because WebBluetooth packets arrive in 12-sample (46.875 ms) bursts and browser draw() loops run at 60 Hz (16.67 ms per frame), software-stamped stimulus triggers have roughly 15 to 45 ms of timing jitter, which "blurs very fast early sensory peaks, though slower responses such as the N170 and P300 are still clear." This is a crucial methodological point that is worth making slightly more concrete for readers. In the frequency domain, convolving an ERP waveform with a uniform jitter distribution of width W = 30 to 45 ms is equivalent to applying a zero-phase low-pass boxcar filter with its first spectral null at f = 1/W (roughly 22 to 33 Hz) and a -3 dB cutoff around 10 to 15 Hz. That immediately explains why a narrow 20 ms auditory brainstem or early C1/P1 peak is strongly attenuated, whereas a broad P300 wave (whose energy is mostly below 5 Hz) passes through with minimal amplitude loss! Adding two sentences explaining this equivalent low-pass cutoff (~10-15 Hz) in Section 6 will give instructors a crisp, quantitative explanation to share with their students.


Section 2. Artifact Rejection vs. Artifact Correction in Classroom Recording

2.1. Explaining Why the >150 microvolt Threshold is Applied to FFT Windows and How Students Handle Blinks in ERP Epochs (Section 2.2 and Section 4.2):
In Section 2.2, you note that the 1-second (256-sample) FFT band-power engine rejects windows where max |x[n] - mean| exceeds 150 microvolts so that eye blinks and muscle spikes do not inflate band power. However, in the online ERP code block in Section 4.2 (examples/epochs.js), every captured epoch is averaged into avgERP without an amplitude rejection check. Because the simulator injects 115 microvolt frontal eye blinks on AF7/AF8 (and real participants blink frequently during visual tasks), a single unrejected blink during the -200 to 0 ms baseline will shift the entire post-stimulus ERP trace by dozens of microvolts, while a blink at +300 ms will distort the P300! Please add a brief note in Section 4.2 (or a 2-line comment in the epochs.js code block) showing how students can add a simple peak-to-peak or max-amplitude artifact rejection check (for example, skipping trials where max - min > 100 microvolts) before adding the trial to avgERP.

2.2. Clarifying the Scope and Limitations of the 6-Tap NLMS IMU Motion Filter on Real EEG Data (Section 4.4 and Section 6):
The 6-tap Normalized Least Mean Squares (NLMS) adaptive filter using the 6-axis IMU as a reference input (Section 4.4 and Figure 5D) is a terrific educational demonstration of Widrow's adaptive noise cancelling. In the synthetic benchmark, where the artifact is generated as a linear combination of the 6 IMU channels, the 6-tap filter achieves 19.2 dB attenuation. On a real dry-electrode headband, however, head movement creates both linear inertial cable/headband sway (which correlates linearly with the IMU at zero lag) and nonlinear electrode-skin impedance shifts or delayed mechanical settling (which require longer multi-delay FIR taps or cannot be fully removed by a 6-tap instantaneous spatial combiner). Please add one or two sentences in Section 4.4 or Section 6 noting that the 6-tap filter models instantaneous linear coupling with the 6 IMU axes (1 tap per axis), and that on physical headsets, sudden electrode slip or delayed mechanical settling may only be partially removed.


Section 3. Psychophysiological Figure Standards and Visual Audit

3.1. Figure 3C ERP Waveform Plotting Conventions (Page 8):
In Figure 3C (Triggered Epoch Extraction and sqrt(K) ERP Convergence), the x-axis is clearly labeled in milliseconds (-200 to 800 ms @ 256 Hz) with a dashed vertical stimulus onset line at 0 ms, and the y-axis is labeled in physical units (Baseline-Corrected Potential in microvolts, positive plotted upward). To make this plot a model for student ERP figures following standard publication guidelines (Keil et al., 2014; Picton et al., 2000), please state in the Figure 3C caption (a) that positive voltages are plotted upward, (b) which simulated channel is displayed (e.g., AF7/AF8 or TP9/TP10), and (c) the exact latency and peak amplitude of the simulated N170 (-6.5 microvolts at +170 ms) and P300 (+9.5 microvolts at +340 ms) components. (Currently, the earlier two-column draft had those microvolt values in the caption, and they were shortened in the simplified pass—putting those exact numbers back into the Figure 3C caption is very helpful without cluttering the main text.)

3.2. Figure 5A and 5B Rounding Check on fNIRS dB Suppression (Page 10):
In Section 4.3 and the Figure 5A caption, the text reports that short-channel regression reduces cortical Delta[HbO2] RMSE from 1.15 micromolar to 0.11 micromolar, corresponding to 20.1 dB suppression. Note that from the unrounded values in your simulation script (approximately 1.148 micromolar and 0.113 micromolar), 20 * log10(1.148 / 0.113) = 20.1 dB, whereas if a reader plugs the rounded two-decimal numbers into 20 * log10(1.15 / 0.11), they get 20.4 dB. Both are completely fine, but mentioning in the caption that 20.1 dB is computed from the unrounded RMS traces avoids any confusion for careful readers.

3.3. Figure 5C Y-Axis Label vs. Physical Units in the Caption (Page 10):
In Figure 5C (52 Hz 6-DOF IMU Orientation and 2nd-Order Kinematics Cascade), the y-axis is labeled "Normalized Kinematic State" (ranging from -1.0 to +1.5), while the caption describes the four traces in their physical units: head tilt angle (pitch), linear acceleration, 1st-order head velocity (cm/s), and 2nd-order 3D head displacement (cm). Because those four physical quantities have different units (degrees, m/s^2, cm/s, and cm), normalizing each trace by its maximum absolute value so they can be overlaid on a single axis is a very sensible visualization choice! Please add a brief phrase in the Figure 5C caption stating that each trace is normalized to unit peak amplitude for visual comparison of phase and integration lag across the kinematic cascade.
